## Summary

Artificial intelligence has become essential to corporate management and national competitiveness, yet the structural limits of generative AI create new risks, such as hallucinations, bias, and infringement. These failures have led to legal disputes, including fabricated case citations, prompting insurers to introduce exclusion clauses in response to rising loss ratios. At the same time, AI risk represents a new growth frontier beyond cyber insurance, with Deloitte projecting the AI insurance market to reach USD 4.8 billion by 2032.

## Excerpts

**1. The Advent of the AI Era**

- AI is fundamentally reshaping how industries produce, accelerating a paradigm shift across society and the economy through intelligent automation and data-driven decision-making.

- According to Bain & Company, 95% of U.S. firms use generative AI in their operations; a McKinsey survey puts the figure at 79% (Bain & Company (2025), “Survey: Generative AI’s Uptake Is Unprecedented Despite Roadblocks”; McKinsey (2025), “The state of AI in 2025”).

- AI adoption has moved past new-technology uptake and become a precondition for corporate management and national competitiveness.

**2. Hallucination, Bias, and Infringement**

- AI is upgrading industry, the economy, and everyday life at large, while creating risks not previously experienced: hallucination, bias, and infringement.

- Related incidents keep occurring: fabricated case citations generated by AI hallucination (Walmart Inc. v. Morgan & Morgan), copyright infringement (Graham v. Prince), and a medical AI chatbot inducing suicide (Raine v. OpenAI).

- Other exposures include Air Canada's chatbot giving incorrect refund-policy guidance (Moffatt v. Air Canada), medical misdiagnosis, algorithmic discrimination, industrial robot malfunctions, and cybercrime including deepfakes.

- Using AI output without verification raises a company's likelihood of legal disputes, which stands out as a risk factor worsening liability insurance loss ratios in particular.

- Hallucination, bias, and infringement are analyzed as inherent limits stemming from imbalanced training data and the structural characteristics of probability-based language generation models.

- Hallucination, the generation of nonexistent information, factually incorrect content, and inaccurate statements, is analyzed as an inherent limit of the statistical inference of generative models rather than a simple operational error.

- The black-box opacity of AI processing and its automated decision structures make it harder to identify the cause of errors.

- While liability attribution for AI incidents remains under debate, professional fields are moving toward imposing strict verification duties on the professionals who use AI.

- In medicine, law, and other professional fields, AI is treated as a decision-support tool; for incidents caused by adopting AI output without verification, the last-mile principle of final judgment assigns primary responsibility to the professional.

- Separately from the professional's verification duty, when a design defect in the AI model or serious bias in the training data is the root cause of an incident, developers and operators are not free from product liability or negligence liability either.

- AI providers add disclaimers such as "AI can make mistakes" to soften their liability for risks inherent in statistical learning systems, but for severe casualties such as a medical AI chatbot inducing suicide, a generic disclaimer has limited effect in excluding liability.

**3. Impact on the Insurance Industry and Responses**

- Unverified use of AI output is worsening liability insurance loss ratios; in response, major insurers including AIG are introducing exclusion endorsements to manage AI-related risk.

- Indiscriminate adoption and use of AI output can worsen loss ratios in cyber insurance as well as professional liability (malpractice liability and E&O), D&O, and commercial general liability lines.

- In response, the U.S. ISO developed an endorsement excluding losses caused by AI errors (Generative AI Exclusion), and major insurers including AIG and Great American are preparing to adopt it.

- The advent of the AI era both threatens insurers' loss ratios and presents an opportunity to open new markets.

- In a survey of companies in the six largest insurance markets (US, UK, Japan, Germany, France, China), more than 90% expressed interest in AI-related insurance; AI adoption rates and insurance demand were both high among digitally mature US and Chinese firms (Geneva Association (2025), “Gen AI Risks for Businesses: Exploring the role for insurance”).

- Deloitte projects the AI-risk insurance market to grow at a high average annual rate of 80%, reaching USD 4.8 billion by 2032 (Deloitte (2025), “AI insurance could be a $4.8B market by 2032”).

- In its risk characteristics, AI-related risk resembles cyber insurance.

- AI insurance and cyber insurance share simultaneous, widespread loss events, information asymmetry, continuous technological progress, and coverage centered on intangible assets.

- Unlike cyber insurance, which mainly covers external attacks such as hacking, AI insurance covers losses arising from internal malfunction, including model defects and hallucination, and lacks standardized risk-assessment metrics.

- Products covering AI hallucination and related risks are at an early stage; development spans extensions of existing products such as cyber insurance and standalone products.

- Munich Re offers its aiSure solution to AI providers and corporate users; Relm Insurance has developed and sells specialized products such as its D.I.C. Policy, covering AI-related liability as well as first-party losses and the coverage gaps of underlying and umbrella liability policies.
