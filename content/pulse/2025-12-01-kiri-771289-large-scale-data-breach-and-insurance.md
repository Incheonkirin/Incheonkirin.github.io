## Summary

The large-scale personal data breach at Coupang in November 2025 was a cyber incident that underscored how cybersecurity failures at Big Tech and digital platform companies with dominant market power can disrupt financial markets, the real economy, and broader social systems. Although the global cyber insurance market, driven largely by the United States, has expanded in response to the rise in cyberattacks and systemic losses, growth in Korea's cyber insurance market has been comparatively slow. Effective management of systemic cyber risk will therefore require coordinated efforts from firms, the insurance industry, and the government.

## Excerpts

**1. The breach**

- Coupang suffered a large-scale personal data breach in November 2025, and controversy followed over the scope of the damage and the manner of disclosure.

- Coupang belatedly confirmed and announced that 33.7 million accounts, effectively its entire user base, had been exposed to unauthorized access.

- The leaked data includes many items tied directly to consumers' daily lives, such as names, contact details, addresses, and purchase histories.

- Fear of secondary harm has driven customer churn, hurting small merchants that depend heavily on Coupang for revenue, and this can also become a burden on the national economy.

- The Coupang breach can be read as a new form of systemic cyber risk, in which a cybersecurity failure at a firm with a monopolistic (or oligopolistic) position triggers second- and third-order damage.

- In a market structure concentrated around Big Tech and platforms, an incident in which a dominant firm fails at cybersecurity and the personal data of most of the population leaks is systemic in character: the shock extends beyond any single company or industry to the financial, real-economy, and social systems as a whole.

**2. The case for expanding the cyber insurance market**

- In response to increasing cyberattacks and systemic losses, the global cyber insurance market has grown rapidly, led by the United States.

- Before the pandemic, cyber insurance was known as a highly profitable line because claims stayed low relative to policy growth; recently, risk exposure has risen sharply, and with both policy counts and claim counts up substantially, profitability has deteriorated somewhat and rates are rising fast.

- In particular, greater exposure to systemic cyber risk raised risk awareness in the market and increased both supply and demand, while also sharply increasing supply-side concerns about accumulation risk and state-backed cyberattacks.

- To overcome this, calls for policy support to expand the insurance market and for public-private partnership programs have grown across the global market.

- Korea's cyber insurance market is still growing slowly; in particular, the information-security awareness and policies of the firms that would buy cyber insurance are suppressing demand.

- Corporate management needs to recognize the importance of cybersecurity accurately and formalize the governance around it.

- Regulators also need a framework that raises the industry's overall security level: supervising whether firms establish and operate a cyber risk management system within their internal controls, and setting disclosure standards for security investment.

- In addition, liability payouts arising from personal data leaks are small, so firms have little incentive to buy cyber insurance.

- The Personal Information Protection Act's penalty ceiling, applied from this year, was raised to 3% of revenue, but liability awards as actual compensation to affected customers remain very limited; civil liability risk is therefore very low, which can undermine the incentive to buy insurance.

**3. Tasks ahead**

- Responding effectively to systemic cyber risk events, such as large-scale data breaches, cyberattacks on national infrastructure, and network outages caused by ransomware, requires joint effort from firms, the insurance market, and the government.

- Insurers need to establish an identity as comprehensive cyber risk management service providers and strengthen supply capacity, including incident prevention and claims adjustment capability.

- Cyber risk, given its network character, can grow in scale over time and has high potential to spread by triggering second- and third-order damage.

- These traits differ from the risks covered in existing insurance markets and imply large outcome uncertainty when a risk event occurs.

- Insurers should therefore go beyond paying compensation after an incident: actively hire experts to improve information security and update capability, or build risk-management consortiums with cybersecurity firms, in order to raise the likelihood of preventing incidents and to secure pre-incident risk management services that contain the scope of damage when one occurs.

- Cyber claims-adjustment capability, including detecting whether an attack occurred and quickly establishing the scope of damage, is also essential for handling the mandatory breach-notification regime and for public relations.

- Balancing demand (firms) and supply (insurers) in the cyber insurance market and laying a stable foundation for growth requires active public-sector policymaking and support.

- Sanctions need to be strengthened to correct firms' complacent attitude toward personal data protection.

- Along with larger administrative penalties, liability to affected customers should be expanded substantially, raising awareness that failure in cyber risk management can seriously damage a firm's financial soundness and value.

- Also, given that state-backed cyberattacks (or cyber terrorism) are excluded from insurance coverage, a national reinsurance scheme or a public-private insurance program may be needed.

- The U.S. Treasury included cyber insurance covering cyber liability in the existing Terrorism Risk Insurance Program (TRIP), and the U.K. government likewise extended its existing reinsurance program that assumes terrorism risk from insurers (Pool Re) to cover corporate property damage and business-interruption losses from cyber terrorism (see Song Yun-ah and Hong Bo-bae (2021), 주요국 정부의 사이버보험 시장 참여 배경 및 동향 (Background and Trends of Government Participation in Cyber Insurance Markets in Major Countries), Issue Report, Korea Insurance Research Institute)

- Financial authorities need to define cyber risk as a systemic risk and build stress-test models for it, applying them to estimate the financial damage that a systemic cyber incident could trigger, covering financial and insurance companies as well as Big Tech and large digital platform operators.
