## Summary

Corporate use of generative AI is growing, the associated risks are expanding, and interest in generative AI insurance products is rising. Because generative AI risk is hard to measure and its loss ratio hard to manage, some overseas insurers exclude generative AI risk from coverage, while insurers that specialize in covering it have also emerged. Introducing generative AI insurance products in Korea calls for a staged approach: survey insurance demand, align the definition of generative AI with the existing legal and regulatory framework, and introduce products selectively, starting with the risks that are easiest to underwrite.

## Excerpts

- Corporate use of generative AI is growing quickly and the associated risks are growing with it, so interest in insurance products that cover generative AI risk is rising.

- According to a Bain & Company survey,[^1] about 95% of US companies have already adopted generative AI in their operations, and over 80% of the companies using generative AI reported that they achieved their intended purpose.

- At the same time, risks are increasing: false information generated and spread through hallucination, copyright infringement, learned social discrimination, and deepfakes. Companies using generative AI are exposed to these risks.

- Interest in insurance covering generative AI risk is rising accordingly. In a Geneva Association corporate survey,[^2] over 90% of respondents expressed interest in insurance products that compensate for damage arising from generative AI use.

- Generative AI risk is latent, affects many parties at once, and has unclear liability, so it can produce large-scale losses. Risk measurement and loss-ratio management are central to product design.

- Damage from generative AI is unevenly distributed depending on the data, the model, and how users apply it. Delayed emergence and simultaneity make claim frequency and severity hard to predict, so product design requires care.

- Damage is often recognized long after a user has acted on inaccurate generated information, or errors in past outputs are discovered only after new data or models are incorporated into the AI algorithm. Damage can therefore accumulate latently and grow in scale.

- Generative AI can cause similar damage to many users of the same model or algorithm.

- The roles of data providers, model developers, platform operators, and users are intertwined, so when an error occurs, causation and liability are hard to attribute.

- Some insurers have amended their policy wording to exclude generative AI risk from coverage.

- The Insurance Services Office (ISO), a Verisk subsidiary, provides a standard-form endorsement that adds property damage, bodily injury, and personal or advertising injury caused by generative AI[^3] errors to the exclusions.[^4]

- Verisk is a multinational insurance data analytics company that supports insurers' risk management. ISO develops standard policy forms for a range of insurance products from insurance data, presents them as benchmarks, and updates them continuously.[^5]

- National Union Fire Insurance[^6] and Great American Insurance[^7] used Verisk's endorsement to file policy amendments with several US state insurance regulators that exclude compensation for damage caused by generative AI.

- If the ISO endorsement spreads, insurance products that do not compensate for generative AI damage may become common.

- Conversely, insurers covering generative AI risk have emerged, narrowing the coverage gap created by existing insurers' exclusion clauses.

- AXA added an endorsement to its existing cyber insurance that covers data poisoning, regulatory violations, and legal disputes over copyright infringement arising from generative AI use.[^8]

- Munich Re quantitatively assesses a generative AI system's error probability before underwriting to set the rate, and compensates policyholders for financial losses from AI errors and for damages arising from legal liability.[^9]

- Armilla AI, an underwriting insurtech, and China's PICC operate standalone insurance products dedicated to generative AI risk, covering liability damages and legal costs.[^10]

- For the Korean insurance industry to cover generative AI risk, a staged approach appears necessary: first assess insurance demand, set the definition of generative AI and the coverage items to be compatible with the legal and regulatory framework, and pilot products selectively, starting with the risks that are easiest to underwrite.

- Damage cases from Korean companies' use of generative AI and demand for insurance products need to be surveyed.

- According to an Amazon Web Services survey,[^11] 48% of Korean companies have adopted AI in their operations, but about 70% of them use AI at a basic level, such as chatbots or simple repetitive tasks.

- With Korean companies' generative AI adoption still at an early stage, damage cases may be few and demand for related insurance products may be limited.

- The definition of generative AI and the coverage items should be set to be compatible with the Korean legal and regulatory framework.

- Insurance contracts are governed by statutes including the Civil Act, the Commercial Act, and the Insurance Business Act, and insurance products must be filed with or approved by the supervisory authority before launch, so the definition of generative AI and the coverage items in policy wording must also be compatible with the Korean legal and regulatory framework.

- Just as Verisk drew on US government materials and the NAIC (National Association of Insurance Commissioners) when preparing the definition of generative AI for its standard forms, Korea can reference the definition of 'generative artificial intelligence' in Article 2, Paragraph 5 of the AI Basic Act (「인공지능기본법」).

- Copyright infringement litigation involving generative AI is also underway in Korea, and coverage items can be made progressively more specific as case law and interpretive direction on generative AI accumulate.

- Among the generative AI coverage items, pilot products can start with the risks that are easiest to underwrite.

- For example, when a loss arises from a generative AI error in a company's internal work processes, the loss is confined to the company's own damage and is not passed on to third parties such as consumers, so setting compensation criteria is relatively straightforward.

- For services provided to a small number of clients, products can be designed to cover losses from client decisions caused by generative AI errors, similar to existing professional liability insurance.

- For copyright infringement disputes, the legal decision structure is relatively simple and the range of settlements or damages is predictable, which can make risk sizing easier.
